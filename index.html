KEKE Madeleines

It began simply because I missed something sweet.

After going keto, dessert became the thing I longed for but hesitated to eat. So I tied on an apron in my own kitchen and tested batch after batch by the soft glow of the oven. Before long, even the furry members of the family had learned to sit in a neat row, eyes bright with anticipation.

Then one day, little shell-shaped cakes rose into golden curves on the baking sheet. The rich aroma of browned butter filled the living room and drew everyone in.

That was how KEKE was born.

Not a grain of refined sugar, yet wrapped in a wonderfully rich aroma.

It is a gentle beginning at the breakfast table and an easy sweetness for afternoon tea. Nourishing and light — made for everyone who loves dessert and chooses to live with care.

Why you will love KEKE madeleines

  • Truly keto, without the burden: Carefully selected low-carb ingredients, with no refined sugar, make it easier to stay in ketosis.
  • The classic French golden curve: Delicately crisp and golden outside, soft and moist inside, with a deep buttery aroma.
  • A gentle everyday ritual: Enjoy one with black coffee at breakfast or unsweetened tea in the afternoon.

Serving suggestions

  • Enjoy as is: Thaw at room temperature to taste the pure, rich buttery flavor.
  • Microwave: Warm for 5–8 seconds for an even softer, more tender crumb.
  • Air fryer or oven: Warm at 160°C / 320°F for 2–3 minutes to bring back the just-baked crisp outside and soft center.

EVERYDAY LIFE IN AMERICA

Mid-Autumn Full Moon Night: One Moon, Two Reunions

September 25, 2026

Tonight at ten, while walking the dogs to the end of the street, a wash of warm golden light stopped me in my tracks.

My neighbors had built a sukkah. Strings of tiny lights hung from its roof, their warm glow slipping through the gaps in the palm leaves, while songs and laughter drifted from inside. Today was the first day of Sukkot, and it also fell on Friday evening, Shabbat—no wonder it was so lively.

I stood beneath the streetlight for a while. I could not understand the lyrics, but I understood that kind of joy—the joy of family gathered together to eat, sing, and give thanks for the harvest.

As it happens, today is also our Mid-Autumn Festival. One is Sukkot, one is Mid-Autumn; the names are different, but the dates met, and even their meanings met: gratitude for the harvest, family reunion, and prayers for peace.

Different cultures, different faiths—yet when we look up, we see the same moon. Tonight it was large and round, hanging above the palm trees, so bright that even the streetlights seemed unnecessary.

I thought of Psalm 133: “Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!”

Tonight, the end of our street looked exactly like that verse.

Happy Mid-Autumn Festival. Shabbat Shalom. May the moonlight reach you too, and may the people you love be by your side. 🌕

Small but Mighty: The Mushroom, My Keto Superfood

Mushrooms are the superfood of my keto table — from breakfast to dinner — with real research to back it up.

October 1, 2026

The other day I saw a tweet calling mushrooms an underrated superfood. I couldn't agree more.

I love mushrooms. They're the superfood of my keto table, versatile enough to go from breakfast to dinner: mushrooms in my omelette, tofu and mushrooms as the perfect pair in miso soup, and they work in stir-fried lettuce with eggs too. Mushrooms go with eggs, tofu, meat — pretty much every protein out there; there seems to be nothing they can't pair with. And in a stew? The moment mushrooms go in, the broth turns incredibly savory — so tasty it'd make your eyebrows fall off. Mushrooms even eat like meat, full of umami.

A small handful of mushrooms packs astonishing nutrition. Very low in carbs, so they're keto-friendly, and rich in B vitamins, selenium, potassium, and beta-glucan fiber. Sun-dried mushrooms even contain vitamin D. Their most special compound is ergothioneine, a powerful antioxidant. Mushrooms are the richest dietary source of ergothioneine, especially shiitake, oyster, maitake, and king oyster mushrooms.

A 2021 Penn State meta-analysis pooling 17 studies and nearly 20,000 cancer patients found that eating about 18 grams of mushrooms a day — just a small handful — was associated with roughly 45% lower cancer risk than non-eaters. A National University of Singapore study following 663 adults over 60 for six years found that those eating more than two servings of mushrooms a week had about 50% lower odds of mild cognitive impairment. These are observational studies, but they all point the same way: people who eat mushrooms often end up with a body and brain that's hard to beat.

One thing to remember: for safety and easier digestion, it's best to cook mushrooms thoroughly before eating!

I eat mushrooms almost every day. For a mushroom lover like me — why not every day.

This Name Should Have Been Changed Long Ago.

Trump wants to rename Artificial Intelligence to Super Intelligence; Newsom fired back with an executive order. I thought about it seriously: this name should have been changed long ago.

October 1, 2026

The backstory

Trump signed an executive order requiring the federal government to say Super Intelligence instead of Artificial Intelligence; California governor Newsom immediately signed an order declaring that artificial intelligence shall permanently be called artificial intelligence, captioned “You're welcome, America.”

But the rename itself, I think, deserves a serious discussion.

ARTIFICIAL comes with a built-in hint of “fake”.

The word artificial carries an undertone of fake, of knockoff — an artificial flower is a fake flower. And today's AI already surpasses humans in many areas. To keep calling it imitation, knockoff intelligence is neither accurate nor fair.

SUPER means superb, not above us.

Super isn't about rising above humanity — it's superb: like a superhero, extraordinary, full of infinite possibility.

In these years of anxiety, depression, and overflowing negativity, a little brightness is exactly what humanity is missing. Many people feel both fond of AI and uneasy about it — this rename opens up room for imagination.

And “super” is such a morale boost; it just lifts your mood.

Habitual thinking makes people resist any renaming.

But without change, there would be no today.

This name should have been changed long ago.

PPK：我想要的老去

PPK，是日本老人最温柔的一句祝愿：好好地活，自己说了算，体体面面地走。

2026年10月4日

PPK，念作 pīn·pīn·kē·luò·lǐ（ピンピンコロリ），是日本老人之间最温柔的一句祝愿：活着的时候ピンピン——生龙活虎，自己说了算；走的时候コロリ——干干净净，不卧床，不拖累。

它的反面叫ネンネンコロリ（nèn·nèn·kē·luò·lǐ）：在床上躺三五年，日子靠家人和护工一天天续着。这是所有人都不想要的结局。

我对日本，不只是尊重，是崇尚。他们的长相、生活习惯，总让我觉得亲切。大概因为同是亚洲人，只隔着一片海——那片海把日本塑成了一种很特殊的存在：面孔与我们相似，骨子里却长出了另一套东西。

比如他们的"两面性"。有人说两面派是坏词，我不觉得。人人都有两面，能把两面都活得体面，是本事。我看过一部日本电影：一位主妇正生着气，电话铃响，她拿起话筒的一瞬间——嘴角上扬，毕恭毕敬，笑容可掬。明明对方看不见她，她整个人的气场却全变了；日本人多爱鞠躬啊，隔着电话线，她仿佛也在深深鞠躬。我敏锐地觉察到：那不是虚伪，是一种体面——把情绪留给自己，把温柔留给别人。

在美国读语言学校时，我有个日本同学。她的英语口音很重，可她从没觉得这是问题。我们中国人常因口音羞于开口，怕人笑话；她没有这种内耗。在我的刻板印象里，日本人多是小家碧玉，可她通透得很。这又是他们的另一面。

我儿子的姑姑，在日本留学、工作，如今生活在中国，却计划去日本养老——把人生的最后一段托付给日本。

在我心里，日本是人间一种近乎完美的存在。国土很小，人却独立，很有个性。年纪大了，不指望孩子天经地义地照料自己——他们不觉得这是应该的。八九十岁，自己看医生，自己过日子，拼命锻炼身体，努力不成为任何人的负担。这其中即使也有无奈，这份自觉依然可敬。

日本并非基督教国家，可他们传福音不找教室、不租礼堂，偏爱小小的咖啡馆。三五个人，点上咖啡，轻声唱起赞美诗——像家庭教会，又护着彼此的隐私，不叨扰谁的家。信仰就着咖啡香，坐在日常里。我太喜欢这种感觉了。

一个人把健康放在心上，受益的是所有人，也是对自己负责。手里永远握着主动权。真到哪天要靠别人照料——我是白羊座，光想想都觉得难。

看着先生一天天辛苦，我照顾他是心甘情愿的，一点不苦。因为他和我是一种人：极不愿麻烦别人。十五年前，医生就预判他很快会需要坐轮椅；可直到今天，他仍然不需要。

所以，PPK，我举双手赞成。这不是口号，是我发自内心想要的体面人生：好好地活，自己说了算，体体面面地走。

KETO & KITCHEN

From Store-Bought Yogurt to Living Cultures: The Naturally Aged Aroma I Brew in My Kitchen

Every night at eight, a small ritual: straining, feeding, and a cup of kefir that stretches like silk.

October 5, 2026

Every night at eight, when the kitchen lights spill across the quartz countertop, comes the quietest — and most looked-forward-to — moment of my day.

Tonight, as usual, I opened the glass jar that had been fermenting all day. What greeted me was a milky-white firmness, dense as soft tofu. I knew my live milk kefir grains had completed another beautiful cycle of living metabolism.

Saying goodbye to passersby, meeting true living symbiosis

For a long time, I drank store-bought bottled kefir, even using commercial yogurt as a starter to keep fermenting batch after batch in my kitchen. Back then I thought yogurt tasted about like that — blunt, one-note sourness.

Until recently, when I finally made up my mind and brought home true, natural, live kefir grains.

I had assumed caring for live cultures would be fussy, and feared I might kill them by accident. But once I started spending two minutes a day with them, I found them far hardier than I had imagined. More surprising still was the flavor they gave back — it completely overturned what I thought yogurt could be.

The first sip of kefir I had strained with my own hands stopped me short. This was no flavor industrial production could replicate — a faintly boozy, jiuniang-like warmth, the naturally matured (Natural Mature), grainy mellowness of a sourdough starter.

That smooth texture, flowing with a slight stretch, is proof of kefiran — the natural polysaccharide secreted by a healthy colony. From nothing but the simplest A2 whole milk, dozens of natural lactic-acid bacteria and wild yeasts, gestated together, had produced such rich and quiet layers of flavor.

The eight o'clock ritual: straining, feeding, nourishing

Tonight at eight, I recorded this small ritual that belongs to Casa Yun:

Harvest by straining: pour the fermented yogurt into a fine stainless-steel mesh strainer and nudge gently with a wooden spoon; the thick, smooth, living kefir flows into the bowl.

Born again: the grains left in the strainer need no rinsing — scoop them straight back into a clean glass jar.

Fresh source: pour in two cups of fresh A2 whole milk, and let the grains, fed for a full day, rest on in a safe home.

Gentle watch: cover loosely and set quietly in a corner. Over the next 24 hours they will keep growing in secret, preparing my next cup of vibrant natural nourishment.

To the strained cup I like to add chia seeds, black sesame powder, sweet almonds and a few nuts. It is not only a golden keto, blood-sugar-friendly breakfast — it is the most direct deep repair for gut and body.

My health, my call

As Florida's weather slowly cools, my body seems to call for a new order. The small ailments and fatigue of the past are behind me; it is time to pick up exercise again, embrace nature, and let the body glow anew from the inside out.

Today I even snuck a selfie. I want to record this starting point — to see, nourished by living cultures, in the daily rhythm of exercise and self-love, what a renewed self might look like in two weeks, a month, half a year.

This tiny culture grows every day; in time it will ferment faster and yield more. I am already looking forward to the day the grains are strong enough to leaven a wheat-scented "Casa Yun aged kefir sourdough loaf" — and to sharing this natural treasure of health with more friends who love life.

Health was never a distant slogan. It is the everyday sizzle of the kitchen, every bite that nourishes the body — the quiet confidence of taking charge of one's own health.

Welcome to Casa Yun. Let us slowly age into better selves, together, as the years pass.

Press Toward the Mark

Preface | For everyone still on the road

For everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under heaven. — Ecclesiastes 3:1

At half past five in the morning, I sit and wait for the sun to rise.

The sky is not yet fully light. All is quiet around me, as if the world too were waiting for a signal — a reminder: a new day has come again.

I often think: for everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under heaven. A time to set out, a time to stay; a time to care for others, and a time to turn around and care for oneself. We always thought life depended on one burst of effort; only later did we understand that what truly carries a person far is walking forward, a little, every day.

I am a highly sensitive person — Highly Sensitive Person, HSP for short. We who are sentimental rather than cold-hearted are like a band of born “frontline sentinels”: we see sooner than others, hear faster, and catch the emotions coming from all directions more easily. This sensitivity is a gift, and it can also become a burden. So I often remind myself: “Mission first, feelings aside.” MISSION FIRST DOES NOT CARE ABOUT FEELINGS.

This isn't about turning cold. In an age of information overload and fragmentation, it's about learning to set down the weight that was never mine to carry. Decluttering emotions isn't heartlessness — it's self-rescue. And not confusing the online world with real life: that's my daily required course.

But no matter how noisy the world gets, I am still moved by the sun rising each day, and I still tend with care the ground beneath my feet. Casa Yun probably grew, slowly, out of mornings like these.

Before it ever becomes a real house, it is first a direction in my heart: on the long pilgrim road, to keep for the weary a clean bed, a breakfast, a hot coffee — and a little quiet that needs no explaining. Travelers can stop here, sort their packs and sort their hearts; when daylight comes, shoulder their stories again and keep walking.

Speaking of “keeping walking” — every time I read that line in Philippians, “press toward the mark,” I can't help but laugh. Growing up in a Chinese-speaking world, I often heard such ringing, forceful phrases and always assumed they came from some propaganda slogan. Only later did I learn that “press toward the mark” was in the Bible all along; even “there is nothing new under the sun” was written long ago in Ecclesiastes. In that moment I was both stunned and delighted: the sentences that grew up with us turn out to be older than we thought — and to have a deeper source.

And “press toward the mark” is such a vivid image. In my mind there's always a person who sees the mark, throws caution aside, charges — charge, charge — and then, bang, runs straight into it. Every time I picture it, I laugh. Maybe faith was always allowed this kind of humor: solemn need not be stiff, and steadfast need not wear a frown. The mark isn't there to crash into; it's there to calibrate our direction. What we're called to do isn't to outrun everyone, but to still know where we're headed, even as we stumble along.

Every journey has a beginning, and will have an end. But the future is worth looking forward to, and hope keeps flowing. Casa Yun is not the destination — more like a lamp by the roadside: reminding everyone who comes here that rest is not stagnation, and setting out again need not wait until everything is ready.

So — charge. With faith, and with laughter; press toward the mark, but remember not to actually crash into it.
