PPK — pronounced pīn·pīn·kē·luò·lǐ (ピンピンコロリ) — is the gentlest wish passed among Japan's elderly: while alive, ピンピン — vibrant and healthy, in charge of oneself; when the time comes, コロリ — clean and quick, no bedridden years, no burden on anyone.

Its opposite is ネンネンコロリ (nèn·nèn·kē·luò·lǐ): lying in bed for three, five years, with the days sustained by family and caregivers. Nobody wants that ending.

My feeling for Japan goes beyond respect — it is admiration. Their faces, their way of life, always feel familiar to me. Perhaps because we are both Asian, separated by only a sea — that sea shaped Japan into something unique: faces like ours, yet something entirely different underneath.

Take their “two-facedness.” Some say being two-faced is a flaw; I disagree. Everyone has two sides — living both of them with grace is a skill. I once watched a Japanese film: a housewife in the middle of being angry; the phone rings, and the instant she picks up — wow, the corners of her mouth lift, polite to a fault, all smiles. The caller cannot even see her, yet her whole presence transforms; you know how the Japanese love to bow — through the phone line, she seemed to be bowing deeply. I sensed it sharply: this was not hypocrisy, it was grace — keeping the feelings to oneself, leaving gentleness for others.

At language school in America, I had a Japanese classmate. Her English carried a heavy accent, but she never saw it as a problem. We Chinese often stay silent for fear our accent will draw laughter; she had none of that inner friction. In my stereotype, Japanese people are delicate and reserved — yet she was utterly clear-eyed and at ease. Another side of them.

My son's aunt studied and worked in Japan. She lives in China now, but plans to retire in Japan — entrusting the final chapter of her life to Japan.

In my heart, Japan is close to a perfect existence in this world. A tiny country, yet its people are independent and full of character. Growing old, they do not expect their children to care for them as a matter of course — they do not see it as owed. At eighty, ninety, they see doctors on their own, live on their own, exercise hard, striving not to become anyone's burden. Even if some of it is necessity, that self-awareness alone commands respect.

Japan is not a Christian country, yet when they share the gospel they do not look for classrooms or rent halls — they favor tiny coffee houses. Three or five people, coffee ordered, softly singing hymns — like a house church, yet guarding each other's privacy, disturbing no one's home. Faith, steeped in the aroma of coffee, sitting inside ordinary days. I love that feeling so much.

![Ghibli-style illustration of the street where the café stands](assets/ppk-cafe-02.jpg)

When a person takes their health to heart, everyone benefits — and it is also being responsible for oneself. You always hold the initiative in your own hands. If the day ever comes when I must depend on others for care — I am an Aries; the mere thought is hard to bear.

Watching my husband struggle more each day, I care for him willingly, without a trace of bitterness. Because he and I are the same kind of person: deeply unwilling to trouble others. Fifteen years ago, his doctor predicted he would soon need a wheelchair; to this day, he still doesn't.

So, PPK — I raise both hands in favor. This isn't a slogan; it's the dignified life I want from the bottom of my heart: live well, stay in charge, and leave with grace.
