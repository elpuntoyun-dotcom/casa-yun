In life, we often fall into a strange loop: longing to move forward, yet feeling our steps unbearably heavy; wanting to embrace the sunlight, yet forever shrouded in inner gloom.

We feel wronged, we complain, we rage — replaying over and over in our minds the moments we were hurt, betrayed, misunderstood. We think we're demanding justice, think we're holding our principles, but in truth we're only locking ourselves inside the prison of victimhood.

Have you ever considered that what truly traps you was never the cruel outside world, but that rotten rope you clutch so tightly, unwilling to let go?

Imagine: if the essence of a human being were a balloon, then rising upward would be its physical inevitability. It was born to belong to the vast sky, to embrace the warm sunlight. Yet why do so many spend their whole lives suffocating and spinning in the deep water? Not because you didn't try hard enough, not because your gifts were meager, not because fortune never arrived — but because your ankles are tied with heavy weights: fear, resentment, disappointment, and an attachment to the identity of victim.

These weights are like thick, rotten ropes, binding you to the dark, icy seafloor.

Playing the "victim" holds a hidden temptation: it lets us believe everything is someone else's fault, so we can righteously stand still, so we can justifiably complain about life. Yet the price of this attachment is extraordinarily steep — every extra second of anger, every extra moment of grievance, wounds only your present life. You are paying enormous rent to ghosts long dead.

Ten seconds ago is the past. Ten hours ago is the past. Ten years ago is likewise the past. In this physical world, the past does not exist at all. If you don't cut the rope, no matter how you struggle and thrash, you will never surface.

Ephesians 4:26 says: "Do not let the sun go down on your wrath." This is not merely moral instruction — it is an exquisitely precise law of life preservation and self-protection.

Why "sunset"? Because sunset marks the boundary of a day. When the sun goes down, the old day is thoroughly over. Carrying anger and resentment through the night is like injecting yesterday's poison into today's blood, letting yesterday's haze pollute tomorrow's morning light. The Bible reminds us: emotions are allowed, but they must be given a strict deadline.

When your partner infuriates you, your business partner stabs you in the back, or life lands a heavy punch — if you let the emotion ruminate inside you, if you let the anger cross the sunset, you have already completely lost that round. Because you have handed the sovereignty of your life to the one who hurt you.

When you can't get out, you don't need to change the current, nor do you need to defeat the one who hurt you. What you truly need is a pair of soul scissors. These scissors represent a decisive will — an absolute defense of sovereignty over your own life.

The moment you catch yourself complaining again, ruminating over grievances, stop and say to yourself: "This is a rotten rope. I'm paying rent to the past again." Don't try to wrestle in the mud with the people and things that hurt you — lingering in battle is itself a form of sinking. True winning is never giving the other party the chance to drag you into their game.

Take out your soul scissors and cut the cord cleanly. No lingering, no ruminating, no looking back.

Cutting the rope does not mean forgiving the other's wrongs — it means choosing to free yourself. When you no longer feed the past with your emotions, when you fully release that rotten rope you've been clutching until it hurt, you will discover that you don't need to struggle desperately at all — cut it, and rising to the surface is simply your life's physical inevitability.
